Trying Perplexity

Nov 27, 2026

Preface

I set Perplexity as my homepage, so every time I open a new tab I'm forced to use whatever chat engine is sitting there — in this case, Perplexity. I'd used it like once before. I didn't even know it had a chat function. It did.

This was a cursory test, not a deep dive. My initial knee-jerk reaction: it felt less capable than ChatGPT. The chat is linked below.

First impressions

It was quick. The responses came fast, and that's good. But when I asked it about stocks, it was just kind of like, I can't do that. Whereas ChatGPT was like, oh yeah, here, this doc, here's this thing — connecting to external sources, rendering mock-ups and charts, creating charts. Perplexity felt a bit more one-dimensional. I know it's supposed to be for searching the web or something, but it felt like there was kind of less there.

Once I pushed it, it did suggest some stocks. So it wasn't a refusal, exactly — more that it took pushing to get anything out of it, where ChatGPT volunteered the richer stuff unprompted.

The chat

The session was seven exchanges, 4:05–4:09pm on Nov 27. It started with a hello and a bit of poking around, and Perplexity was fast — one reply came back in a second.

I asked whether it could research the market and recommend a specific call option for me to buy. It said no to the specific pick: it doesn't have access to my brokerage account or risk profile, and a wrong guess could lose the whole premium. What it offered instead was research on an underlying stock if I gave it a ticker and a timeframe, plus the questions it would need first — what stock or sector, what outlook and timeframe, and how much I could afford to lose.

Then I asked whether Perplexity is developing its own LLMs. It said sort of: it mainly post-trains open-source models rather than pretraining frontier models from scratch. Sonar is its in-house family, tuned for search, with the newer version built on Llama 3.3 70B and trained with NVIDIA NeMo, ready within days of a new open-source release. R1 1776 is its post-trained version of DeepSeek-R1, with a distilled Llama 70B version too. Its newer Agent API also routes to models from OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, xAI and others. It didn't know which specific model was answering me.

When I pushed — just suggest a stock, please — it still wouldn't give a buy pick, but it did show me the most active US stocks that day, the day after Thanksgiving, when volume was thin. Semiconductors were driving the day: NVIDIA at $150.49, up 0.80%; Intel at $57.40, up 5.38%; SanDisk at $85.36, up 15.96%; and the leveraged semiconductor ETFs SOXL at $36.57, up 14.21%, and SOXS at $52.94, down 14.79%. It called NVIDIA the steadiest name on the list, warned me off the leveraged pair because they reset daily, and said big one-day jumps like SanDisk's often mean the easy gains are already in.

Finally I said I just wanted to test the waters and explore options trading. It gave me a beginner primer: what calls and puts are, that the premium is the most you can lose on a bought option and it can go to zero, and the risks — time decay, volatility crush after events like earnings, leverage, thin liquidity, cheap out-of-the-money long shots, and not selling uncovered calls. Then the starting steps: get broker approval, read the risk disclosure, paper trade first, start with a single contract, decide an exit plan in advance, and size it small.

That last part was the most useful thing in the session, and also the most generic — a solid explainer, not a trading assistant.

Notes

  1. Speed is the strong point. Responses were quick, noticeably so.
  2. Depth is the weak point. Asked about stocks, the first answer was effectively a shrug; the useful part only came after I pushed.
  3. Compared with ChatGPT, the contrast was the artifacts: ChatGPT produced documents, mock-ups, charts. Perplexity produced text.
  4. Usage limits are unknown to me. It seems fine so far. I ran into usage limits with Perplexity before, months ago, and I'd guess they still have some sort of limit — probably a cutoff after something like 20 messages a day — but I don't actually know.
  5. One-dimensional is the word that kept coming to mind. For a product whose whole pitch is searching the web, I expected it to feel like more, not less.

Reflection

The homepage trick is doing real work here: it forces the comparison. Opening a tab and getting Perplexity instead of ChatGPT makes the gap visible in a way a deliberate test wouldn't. ChatGPT feels like an environment — it goes and gets things, makes things, shows you things. Perplexity feels like a search box that talks back. That might be unfair after one short session, and I want to do a deeper dive before I settle the verdict. But the first impression is the first impression: quick, fine, and nothing to write home about.

Conclusion

It was okay. Quick responses, some stock suggestions once pushed, no limits hit yet. But next to ChatGPT it felt less capable, and I don't yet know whether that's the product or just my one short session. Deeper dive pending.
