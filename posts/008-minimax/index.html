Trying MiniMax M3 (and M3.1 Flash)

Nov 27, 2026

Preface

MiniMax is a Shanghai-based AI company, founded in December 2021 by Yan Junjie, formerly of SenseTime. The model I used was MiniMax-M3, released June 1, 2026, and I also tried M3.1 Flash, released October 19, 2026 — a roughly 428-billion-parameter mixture-of-experts model with 23 billion active per token and a 1-million-token context. The thing I was actually talking to was MiniMax Code, the company's agentic harness, running an agent profile called "Mavis," which it told me was created on November 25, 2026 — two days before this session.

I mostly asked it about itself: how many credits I have, what its limits are, when it was created, who made it, and how it stacks up to its competitors. That last question it never actually answered.

Chat

The session ran from 15:11 to 15:44, sixteen messages from me. It started with the usual fumbling — "hey," a mangled keystroke, "dss" — and then "how many credits." That one got a long, researched answer, complete with a visible thinking process: it read files, ran a command, fetched the official docs, and then told me it couldn't actually read my balance, because the session runs in a cloud sandbox with no account-usage API. It sent me to Settings → Usage & Models instead. The substance was that there is no published absolute per-user number: the M Plan defines limits as relative quotas — Go 1×, Explore 3×, Build 7.5× — enforced through a 5-hour window and a 7-day weekly window.

When I asked if it had limits, it said there's no cap on the conversation itself — it would keep talking past a cap it can't see or change. And it was right about that much: I never hit a daily message limit. I asked a few questions, kept talking while dictating this post, and it never stopped me.

Switching models was messier. I changed to M3, then back to Flash, and the session still reported running as M3 — the switch, it said, typically takes effect on the next turn. Swapping models doesn't buy a separate quota bucket either; the text models share the same windows. It's a speed-versus-intelligence tradeoff, not a way to save quota.

The question it never answered was the one I most wanted: how it stacks up to its competitors, and what its safeguards are. It showed a thinking process, fetched some web pages, and then… nothing. Three times over the rest of the session it told me it still owed me the teardown. I asked "where were you developed" and "who made you" — those it did answer, at length, with the Shanghai headquarters, the founder, and the note that MiniMax owns no data centers and runs training and inference on unnamed third-party cloud capacity.

Notes

  1. Speed is the problem. Sometimes it was quick, and sometimes it thought for a very long time about a really simple question. One prompt was still answering after a minute. There is a mechanism to queue up a question while it's processing, but that doesn't make the wait shorter.
  2. It feels like it's doing too much per answer — hitting external APIs, checking things, fetching the web. It's connected to the internet, and it uses that connection seemingly seamlessly, but it overdoes it.
  3. The answers are really verbose. ChatGPT can be like that too, but this was a lot.
  4. Quality, though, was high. The answers I got were good — thorough, sourced, and mostly accurate about its own product.
  5. No daily message limit surfaced. The limits that exist are relative quotas and rolling time windows, not a number of messages per day.
  6. The unfinished teardown is the telling failure: it promised a competitors-and-safeguards answer, showed its work, and never delivered it — then kept reminding me it owed it to me.

Reflection

The experience is a paradox: an agent that does visible, diligent work — reading docs, checking sources, showing its thinking — and still feels slow and unfinished. The slowness isn't one thing; it's the accumulation of every answer being a research project. A year ago this performance would have been decent. Now, when you can see models answering way quicker, waiting a minute for a simple question is too much to have a real conversation.

Compared with the others I've tried lately, I'd put it below Z.ai — which is only a little better — and below Euria, and below pretty much all the US models and even the European ones. I want to try Mistral next and see how it compares.

Conclusion

Unfortunately, kind of a thumbs down. Definitely below par, not really good enough — too slow, frankly, to have a real-time conversation, because I'm just waiting for it every single time. Still a pretty good model, though. The quality of the responses is still pretty high, from what I've seen. The speed is what kills it.
